傾盤：規則設計與公平性檢查

2026-10-30 · 罡曜第五款原創棋 · 回到遊戲

  • 開局是和棋。電腦把所有 10,852,800 個局面都算過：從這個開局，雙方都下最好時，沒有人能強迫取勝。開局轉半圈後完全相同，所以誰先走都一樣。先走的一方有 8 種第一手，3 種保持均勢；另外 5 種會輸，最快的一種讓對手下一手就贏。
  • 前幾版不夠好，所以改了。第一版的自然開局全是和棋，而且第一手怎麼走都安全。只能傾斜、不能走棋時，對局要拖一百多手。拿掉「不能馬上傾斜回去」，最長的強制勝利只剩 10 手。
  • 雙方程度相近時，勝負接近一半一半。模擬中雙方每步有 10% 機會隨機走錯：先手勝 49.7%，後手勝 50.3%，一盤平均約 28 手。
  • 這是一款很尖銳的棋。大部分局面裡，輪到的一方都有必勝的走法。只看一步的玩家對上完美下法，輸掉 99.8% 以上的對局，平均約 10 手就結束。

1. 怎麼檢查的

棋盤有 25 格，扣掉 2 根石柱和 2 個洞，還有 21 格。每方 3 枚棋子的擺法有 1,330 × 816 = 1,085,280 種，再乘上「對手剛往哪個方向傾斜」（5 種，包括沒有）和輪到誰，共 10,852,800 個局面。電腦從有棋子已經落洞的局面往回推，一層一層標出每個局面在雙方都下最好時的結果：輪到的一方會贏、會輸，或沒有人能強迫取勝（和棋）。這種做法叫逆推分析（retrograde analysis）。4 核心約 5 秒完成。

交叉檢查

  1. 兩套程式數走法：網頁上的規則引擎（JavaScript）和求解器（C）分開撰寫，連棋子怎麼滑也各寫一份。從開局數到第 6 手的所有走法序列（8、57、438、3,419、26,045、193,795）完全相同。
  2. 逐一驗算：由 JavaScript 引擎在 20,000 盤隨機對局中走到的 230,437 個局面，自己產生每一步的後續局面，再檢查表裡的結果是否正好由後續局面推得出來（包括幾手之內分出勝負）。0 個不一致。

2. 規則和棋盤怎麼定出來

我們解了 1,280 種棋盤和規則的組合：10 種石柱擺法、8 種洞的位置、每方 2 或 3 枚棋子、能不能走一格、4 種「不能馬上傾斜」的限制。下表是其中幾個代表。

比例算的是輪到薄荷、沒有禁止的方向、還沒分出勝負的局面。「必勝」包含下一手就能讓棋子落洞的局面。

能走一格，讓防守有了細部的手段：傾斜是一次搬動全盤的大動作，走一格是用來擺好擋位的小動作。「不能馬上傾斜回去」和借路、轉環、光路的同類規則一樣，讓一手棋的效果至少留到下一回合。另一種限制「不能往同一個方向再傾斜」其實不起作用：剛往某個方向傾斜過，再往同方向傾斜什麼都不會動。

3. 開局怎麼選

開局限定為公平的形狀：棋子的擺法轉半圈後不變，石柱和洞也互為轉半圈，所以先手和後手面對的是同一個棋盤。我們再只留雙方棋子都在自己半場（薄荷在第 1、2 列）、第一手至少 4 種、而且有安全也有會輸的走法的和棋開局，共 5 個：

4. 看得深有沒有用

一個只看一步的玩家：能馬上讓自己的棋子落洞就走，不走會讓對方下一手獲勝的棋，其餘隨機。它對上完美下法各 4,000 盤：完美下法執先手勝 99.9%，執後手勝 99.8%，平均約 9 到 10 手就結束。這款棋的勝負常常在兩三步內決定，看得越深，越少吃虧。

5. 電腦對手

電腦用 alpha-beta 搜尋，搜尋量固定（不看時間），所以同一個局面永遠回同一手。下表用完整的結果表打分：隨機抽 300 個輪到電腦、而且還沒輸定的局面，看它的一手有沒有保住原本的結果（必勝仍是必勝，和棋至少還是和棋）。

難度保住結果每步時間（Node）
簡單95.3%不到 1 毫秒
普通98.3%不到 10 毫秒
困難100.0%約 0.2 秒，最多 0.5 秒

簡單難度只看兩步：不會送對手一步就贏的機會，但看不到更遠的陷阱。困難難度對上完美下法下了四盤（執薄荷或珊瑚、先走或後走），四盤都以三次重複和棋。

6. 限制

  • 這款棋很尖銳：81% 的局面裡輪到的一方有必勝，犯一次錯通常馬上被懲罰。對新手來說可能輸得很快。
  • 電腦對電腦時，雙方常常很快走回同一個局面而和棋。真人對局會不會也這樣，要靠試玩才知道。
  • 模擬裡的失誤是隨機走一步，和真人的失誤不同。模擬沒有套用三次重複判和，改用 300 手上限。
  • 只解了 5 × 5，也只試了上面說的 1,280 種組合。
  • 我們沒有找到「整個棋盤傾斜、所有棋子一起滑」的雙人策略棋；最接近的 Slipe 和 Deblockle 都只滑動單一棋子。這只是有限的搜尋，不能證明沒有前例。
  • 「和棋」是指雙方都下最好時的結果，不代表實戰常常和棋。

Qingpan: how the rules were made, and how fair they are

2026-10-30 · GambitAura original game 05 · Back to the game

  • The opening is a draw. A computer worked through all 10,852,800 positions: from this start, with best play on both sides, nobody can force a win. The opening looks the same turned half way round, so it makes no difference who moves first. The first player has 8 first moves; 3 keep the balance, and the other 5 lose, the worst one at once.
  • The first rules fell short, so they changed. In the first layout every natural start was a draw, and no first move was wrong. With tilting only and no steps, games dragged past a hundred moves. Without "no tilting straight back", the longest forced win drops to 10 moves.
  • Between equal players, results are close to even. In simulated games where each move has a 10% chance of being random, the first player wins 49.7% and the second 50.3%, in about 28 moves.
  • It is a sharp game. In most positions the side to move has a winning line. A player who looks only one move ahead loses over 99.8% of games against perfect play, usually within about 10 moves.

1. How it was checked

The board has 25 squares; without the 2 pillars and 2 holes, 21 are left. Three pieces each can stand in 1,330 × 816 = 1,085,280 ways. Times "which way the other side just tilted" (5 options, including none) and whose turn it is, there are 10,852,800 positions. Starting from positions where a piece has fallen into a hole, the computer works backwards, marking every position with its result under best play: the side to move wins, loses, or nobody can force a win (a draw). This is called retrograde analysis. It takes about 5 seconds on 4 cores.

Cross-checks

  1. Two programs count moves: the rules engine on the page (JavaScript) and the solver (C) were written separately, down to the code that slides the pieces. They agree on every move sequence from the opening to depth 6 (8, 57, 438, 3,419, 26,045, 193,795).
  2. Every value re-derived: for 230,437 positions reached in 20,000 random games, the JavaScript engine generated each position's next positions itself, and the table's result had to follow exactly from theirs, including how many moves until the end. 0 mismatches.

2. How the rules and the board were chosen

We solved 1,280 combinations of board and rules: 10 pillar layouts, 8 hole places, 2 or 3 pieces each, with or without steps, and 4 kinds of "no tilting" limit. The table shows some of them.

Shares count positions with Mint to move, no banned direction, and nothing decided yet. "Wins" includes positions where a piece can be dropped in at once.

Steps give the defence fine control: a tilt is the big move that shifts the whole board, and a step is the small move that sets a block in place. "No tilting straight back" works like the matching rules in Jielu, Zhuanhuan and Guanglu: every move lasts at least until the next turn. The other limit, "no tilting the same way again", never matters: right after a tilt one way, another tilt that way moves nothing.

3. How the opening was chosen

Openings were limited to fair shapes: the pieces look the same turned half way round, and so do the pillars and holes, so both players face the same board. We kept the draws with each side's pieces in its own half (Mint on ranks 1 and 2), at least 4 first moves, and both safe and losing ones among them. That leaves 5:

Each start was simulated over 20,000 games, good to about plus or minus 0.7 points. Coral's pieces stand a half turn away.

c2 d2 c1 was chosen for three reasons. Of these starts it is the closest to even and gives the longest games. The first move needs thought (only 3 of 8 hold). And the three pieces start close together, so tilts push them into each other, which shows what the game is about.

4. Does thinking ahead pay?

A player who looks one move ahead drops a piece in when it can, avoids moves that let the other side win next move, and otherwise moves at random. Against perfect play, over 4,000 games each way, perfect play won 99.9% moving first and 99.8% moving second, usually within 9 to 10 moves. Games here are often decided two or three moves deep, and the further you look, the less you lose.

5. The computer player

The computer uses alpha-beta search with a fixed amount of search (not a clock), so a position always gets the same reply. It was scored against the full table: in 300 random positions where the computer is to move and not already lost, did its move keep the result (a win stays a win, a draw stays at least a draw)?

LevelKeeps the resultTime per move (Node)
Easy95.3%under 1 ms
Normal98.3%under 10 ms
Hard100.0%about 0.2 s, at most 0.5 s

Easy looks two moves ahead: it never hands over a win in one, but it cannot see traps further out. Hard played four games against perfect play (as Mint or Coral, moving first or second); all four ended in a threefold-repetition draw.

6. Limits

  • This is a sharp game: in 81% of positions the side to move has a forced win, and a single mistake is usually punished at once. Beginners may lose quickly.
  • In computer games, strong play often returned to the same position quickly and ended in a draw. Whether people play it that way too needs play-testing.
  • A simulated slip is a random move, unlike human mistakes. The simulation does not apply the threefold-repetition draw; it stops at 300 moves instead.
  • Only 5 × 5 was solved, and only the 1,280 combinations described above.
  • We found no two-player strategy game where the whole board tilts and every piece slides; the closest, Slipe and Deblockle, slide single pieces. This was a limited search and does not prove there is none.
  • "Draw" means the result under best play on both sides. It does not mean real games are often drawn.
